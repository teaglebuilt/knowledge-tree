2. DEFINING TSE: MORE THAN JUST EARLY ADOPTION

The term “tech-savvy” often conjures images of individuals effortlessly navigating the latest gadgets or speaking in a lexicon of obscure digital acronyms
(Grover, 2021). While a certain familiarity with technology is undoubtedly
part of the equation, to define TSE merely as “using technology” is akin to
describing a Formula 1 driver as someone who “uses a car.” It dramatically
undersells the strategic depth, anticipatory vision, and systemic integration
required to truly leverage technology for disproportionate entrepreneurial
success in the 21st century.

At its core, TSE is about operating with a proactive, adaptive, and strategic mindset that views technology not as an operational adjunct but as the
fundamental substrate and accelerator for value creation (Hayduk, 2022). It’s
a deep understanding that technological fluency extends beyond mere adoption to encompass a grasp of underlying principles, a foresight into emergent
trends, and the creative capacity to weave these insights into novel business
models and sustainable competitive advantages (FasterCapital, 2025).

Considering the journey of an entrepreneur from our qualitative interviews, who specializes in predictive analytics for niche markets, she isn’t a
software engineer by trade, but her approach to problem-solving is intrinsically technological. She shared,

3. THEORIES OF DIGITAL ENTREPRENEURSHIP AND
THE IMPERATIVE OF TECHNOLOGICAL FLUENCY

The very essence of entrepreneurship – the discovery and exploitation of opportunities (Kirzner, 1979; Shane & Venkataraman, 2000) – has been profoundly
reshaped by the digital age. No longer confined to tangible assets and geographical boundaries, entrepreneurial processes now unfold within an intricate
tapestry of bytes, algorithms, and global networks. To comprehend this transformation, we must delve into the theoretical underpinnings that bridge classical entrepreneurial thought with the emergent realities of the digital economy.

Traditional theories, such as Schumpeter’s (1934) theory of creative
destruction, which posits that innovation drives economic cycles by rendering old structures obsolete, find new resonance in the digital realm. Digital
technologies are the ultimate disruptors, constantly creating new markets
while dismantling old ones at an unprecedented pace. The rise of streaming
services obliterating blockbuster, or ride-sharing apps challenging traditional
taxi monopolies, are modern manifestations of this timeless principle. However, digital creative destruction is often swifter, more far-reaching, and less
predictable than its industrial-era predecessors.

Similarly, the resource-based view (RBV) of the firm (Barney, 1991), which
argues that sustainable competitive advantage stems from rare, valuable,
inimitable, and non-substitutable resources must be re-evaluated in a digital
context. While physical assets remain important, the most potent “resources”
for digital entrepreneurs are often intangible: proprietary algorithms, vast
datasets, network effects, and highly skilled digital talent. The inimitability
now lies in the complexity of integrated digital ecosystems rather than simply
unique machinery.

The academic discourse on digital entrepreneurship has evolved rapidly, giving rise to specific theoretical lenses that illuminate its unique characteristics:

**•** _Network effects theory_ : This theory, popularized by Metcalfe’s (1993) law,
states that the value of a telecommunications network is proportional to
the square of the number of connected users. In digital entrepreneurship,
this translates directly to the power of platforms. The more users on a

10 The Tech-savvy Entrepreneur

social media platform (e.g., Facebook, X), an e-commerce marketplace
(e.g., Etsy, Amazon), or a ride-sharing app (e.g., Uber, Lyft), the more
valuable it becomes to all users – both existing and new. This phenomenon
creates powerful self-reinforcing growth loops and significant barriers
to entry ( _d_ M) for new competitors. An entrepreneur who grasps the
mechanics of direct (value increases with more users of the same type) and
indirect (value increases for one user group as another complementary
group grows) network effects can design business models that achieve
explosive, often viral, growth.

**•** _Platform business models_ : Moving beyond traditional linear value chains,
platform business models leverage digital infrastructure to facilitate
interactions and exchanges between multiple interdependent user groups
(e.g., producers and consumers, drivers and riders, content creators
and audiences) (Parker et al., 2016). Unlike traditional businesses that
create value by producing and selling goods, platforms create value by
enabling connections and transactions (United Nations Conference on
Trade and Development, 2020). This shifts the focus from managing
physical inventory to managing digital communities and data flows. The
scalability of these models is unparalleled; they grow not by investing in
more factories but by attracting more users and leveraging network effects
(Jaworski, 2023).

**•** _The digital economy framework_ : This broad theoretical umbrella
acknowledges that digitalization fundamentally alters economic processes,
resource allocation, and value creation. It highlights characteristics such
as: (1) Once a digital product (e.g., software, music, digital content) is
created, the cost of producing additional copies is near zero. This allows
for massive scalability and profitability once initial development costs are
covered. (2) The internet and digital platforms provide unprecedented
access to information for both consumers and producers, reshaping
market dynamics and power structures. (3) Digital businesses can access
markets and talent pools worldwide from day 1, dissolving geographical
barriers that once constrained entrepreneurial ambition (Abaddi, 2024).
This has profound implications for startups from emerging markets,
enabling them to compete on a global scale.

The COVID-19 pandemic acted as an unprecedented catalyst, compressing years of digital transformation into mere months. It didn’t just accelerate
existing trends; it vividly underscored the existential imperative of technological fluency for entrepreneurial survival and flourishing. As lockdowns forced
businesses online, those lacking digital capabilities faced significant setbacks

4. KEY CONCEPTS IN TSE

In the volatile currents of the digital age, successful entrepreneurship is less
about following a static roadmap and more about navigating by a ­dynamic
compass, constantly recalibrating with every new technological gust. To
­truly master the art of TSE, one must internalize a set of core concepts that
transcend mere buzzwords and become intrinsic modes of operation. These
concepts form the robust framework upon which resilient, innovative, and
market-shaping ventures are built.

4.1. Entrepreneurial Agility

At the heart of TSE lies entrepreneurial agility, which is the profound
capacity of an individual or venture to rapidly perceive, interpret, and
adapt to unforeseen changes and opportunities within the technological
and market landscape. It is not simply about being fast; it is about being
responsive, flexible, and intelligently iterative (Karimi & Walter, 2021). In a
world where a disruptive technology can emerge from a garage startup and
reshape an entire industry within months, static business plans are relics.
Agility becomes the primary determinant of survival and growth (Rocha
et al., 2025).

This agility is fundamentally driven by technological insights. It involves
four elements: (1) Rapid iteration cycles or moving away from lengthy product development cycles toward continuous prototyping, testing, and refinement, often enabled by cloud-based development environments, AI-powered
design tools, and low-code/no-code platforms that drastically reduce time
to market and maintenance. (2) Data-driven pivoting which is the ability to
quickly analyze real-time market feedback, user behavior data, and emerging technological capabilities to make strategic shifts in product, market, or
business model. (3) Decentralized decision-making which means empowering teams with the autonomy and information to make rapid decisions
closer to the problem, rather than relying on slow, hierarchical approvals.
This aligns with principles found in agile software development methodologies (Alsaqqa et al., 2020) now being applied to entire business operations. (4) Learning from failure by viewing failures not as endpoints, but as
rich datasets for learning and adaptation. Each failed experiment, product
iteration, or marketing campaign provides invaluable data points for subsequent, more informed actions.

4.2. Technology Fluency

Technology fluency transcends mere digital literacy. It is the cognitive capacity
to not only operate technological tools but to comprehend their underlying
architectures, anticipate their evolutionary trajectories, and strategically apply
their capabilities to novel business problems. It’s about speaking the language
of technology, not just reading its instructions (Baker & O’Neil, 2003).

This involves several layers of understanding:

**•** _Conceptual mastery_ : Grasping the fundamental principles behind
technologies (e.g., understanding that AI relies on algorithms learning
from data, rather than just using a ChatGPT/Copilot/Gemini interface;
knowing blockchain’s distributed ledger concept beyond cryptocurrency
trading).

**•** _Strategic application_ : Identifying how diverse technologies can converge to
create new value propositions (e.g., combining internet of things sensors
with AI for predictive maintenance, leveraging 6G for truly immersive
metaverse experiences, or using satellite data for precision agriculture).

**•** _Implication foresight_ : Recognizing the ethical, societal, regulatory, and
competitive implications of technological advancements before they
become mainstream. This allows for proactive risk mitigation and
opportunity capture.

**•** _Interoperability understanding_ : Comprehending how different systems and
platforms can or cannot communicate, which is crucial for building scalable
and integrated solutions in an increasingly fragmented digital landscape.

6.1. The Thorny Path: Navigating Challenges in Tech Integration

Bringing cutting-edge tech into a business is rarely smooth – it often comes
with a set of tough challenges that can discourage even bold entrepreneurs.
One of the biggest is the high cost of getting started. Buying into advanced
AI systems, blockchain setups, or metaverse development tools takes serious
money upfront. That price tag can be especially hard for micro-, small-, and
medium-sized enterprises that don’t have much capital to spare. As a startup
founder in the Jordanian fintech space shared during our interviews, “The
vision for a blockchain-powered payment system was clear, but the initial
infrastructure investment felt like building a skyscraper with a shoestring
budget. Access to early-stage funding for deep tech is still a major barrier
here.” This financial burden is compounded by the steep learning curve
(L Curve) associated with mastering complex digital tools and paradigms.
Integrating AI, for instance, demands not just software proficiency but an
understanding of data science principles, model biases, and ethical implications, often requiring significant upskilling or hiring specialized talent –
resources that are scarce and expensive.

Aside from money and skilled people, resistance to change inside the company is a major roadblock. Long-standing routines, fixed ways of thinking,
and natural fear of the unknown can slow down even the best digital transformation plans. Staff may worry about losing jobs to automation, while
leaders might doubt new, untested tech – causing hesitation. On top of that,
the fast-moving tech world brings tricky ethical issues and unclear rules that
add even more complexity. The use of AI raises questions of bias, privacy,
and accountability; blockchain applications grapple with energy consumption and regulatory oversight; and the metaverse introduces new challenges
related to digital identity, data ownership, and virtual criminality. Navigating
this evolving labyrinth of ethical dilemmas and legal frameworks requires
constant vigilance and proactive engagement, adding layers of complexity to
entrepreneurial operations. Indeed, reports from entities like the World Economic Forum (2025) consistently highlight ethical AI and digital trust as critical global challenges that require nuanced leadership.

1. INTRODUCTION

The turn of the decade witnessed an unprecedented global disruption, as the
COVID-19 pandemic unleashed a systemic shock that fundamentally recalibrated socio-economic landscapes worldwide (Abaddi, 2024a). Far from
being a transient anomaly, this crisis served as the “great digital leap” (Δ _G_ ),
compressing years, arguably a full decade, of anticipated digital transformation into a compressed timeframe of reactive necessity. Businesses, industries,
and individuals were compelled to jettison traditional operational paradigms
and embrace digital solutions with an urgency that transcended mere strategic
choice, becoming an existential imperative. The reverberations of this accelerated digitalization continue to redefine the very essence of entrepreneurial
activity, challenging established norms and simultaneously unveiling novel
pathways for value creation (Owusu-Ansah et al., 2024).

Prior to the pandemic, digital transformation was often viewed as a gradual, evolutionary process, a strategic enhancement for competitive advantage.
However, the abrupt and pervasive nature of the COVID-19 crisis transformed this evolution into a sudden, often chaotic, revolution. This rapid,
forced digitalization created a “digital vortex” – a highly dynamic and turbulent environment characterized by unprecedented shifts in customer behavior,
operational methodologies, and market dynamics (Gregurec et al., 2021). For

2.1. The Accelerated Imperative of Digital Transformation

Prior to 2020, scholarly discourse on digital transformation largely focused
on its strategic advantages for competitive differentiation and efficiency gains
(Yucel, 2018). However, the pandemic abruptly elevated it to an existential
imperative, forcing organizations across sectors to rapidly digitize core operations or face severe disruption (Amoah et al., 2021). Research by O’Toole
et al. (2020) starkly illustrates this, reporting that the crisis catalyzed a threeto four-year acceleration in the digitization of customer interactions, supply
chains, and internal operations. This rapid shift was not merely about adopting new tools but about fundamentally rethinking how value is created and
delivered (Dyduch et al., 2021). Literature now emphasizes the concept of
“digital resilience” – the capacity of firms to rapidly adapt and recover from
disruptions by leveraging digital technologies (Chatterjee et al., 2023). This
body of work highlights how cloud computing, often a cornerstone of digital
transformation (Elsersy et al., 2021), became critical for enabling scalability and remote access during lockdowns, shifting from a strategic investment
to a foundational necessity. Consequently, recent academic contributions are
increasingly exploring the organizational and cultural challenges of this compressed transformation, including leadership adaptability, workforce upskilling, and managing employee resistance to rapid technological integration
(Bozkus, 2023; Sarfraz et al., 2024). The literature underscores that successful
acceleration depended not only on technological readiness but also on organizational agility and leadership vision in crisis.

2.2. The Emergence of the “Omni-digital” Consumer
and Market Redefinition

The pandemic profoundly reshaped consumer behavior, fostering an unprecedented reliance on digital channels and fundamentally redefining market
dynamics. This has led to the emergence of what literature now describes as
the “omni-digital” consumer – an individual seamlessly navigating between
online and offline touchpoints, demanding integrated and personalized experiences (Ketron et al., 2025). The surge in e-commerce exemplifies this shift,
with global online retail experiencing growth rates typically seen over several years concentrated into mere months (UNCTAD, 2021). This consumer
behavior shift extends beyond purchasing, encompassing increased engagement with telehealth services, FinTech, digital entertainment, and online education platforms (Abaddi, 2024a).

5.1. Discussion of Results

Our findings offer a detailed look at what entrepreneurship felt like during the
post-COVID-19 digital shift, showing that the pandemic wasn’t just a disruption but a strong digital trigger. The common theme of fast digital transformation, often called a “mandated leap” by participants, supports and expands

a must-do right away. This sudden change challenged the idea that digital
change should be slow and careful and instead showed a new “agile transformation” model shaped by outside shocks. The big spending on digital tools
shared by participants also backs the RBV of the firm (Barney, 1991), where
things like scalable cloud computing and strong cybersecurity became key
resources that can’t easily be copied. These helped firms stand out – or even
survive – in a risky market. The stories show that those who quickly got or

1. INTRODUCTION

We stand at the precipice of a new industrial revolution, one not driven by
steam, electricity, or even microchips alone but by intelligence itself. AI, once
the enigmatic domain of science fiction and academic labs, has transcended
its nascent forms to become the definitive entrepreneurial frontier of the 21st
century. This isn’t merely about automation; it’s about the dawn of augmented
intelligence – a symbiotic partnership between human ingenuity and machine
capability that is fundamentally reshaping the calculus of business value.

The scale of this transformation is staggering. The global AI market, valued at approximately $198 billion in 2023, is projected to soar to $4.8 trillion by 2030, experiencing a 25 _x_ / decade increase (UNCTAD, 2025). This
exponential growth is not just a statistical anomaly; it reflects a pervasive
integration of AI across every conceivable sector. Early adopters report significant gains: businesses leveraging AI are projected to boost productivity
by an average of 25.1% more quickly and 40% higher quality compared to
non-adopters (Vena Solutions, 2024), translating into trillions of dollars in
economic value creation. However, the vast majority aren’t developing ethical
AI policies. A report by Mayer et al. (2025) revealed that +90% of companies
are planning to invest in generative AI in the following three years. For the
tech-savvy entrepreneur, this isn’t just a trend to observe; it’s the very operational substrate upon which competitive advantage will be forged and new
markets will be designed.

The AI landscape has evolved dramatically. Beyond the foundational principles of ML – where algorithms learn patterns from data to make predictions
or decisions (Abaddi, 2025) – we are witnessing the pervasive rise of sophisticated capabilities. NLP agents are now fluent in human communication,
deciphering sentiment and generating nuanced text, while CV systems “see”
and interpret the world with increasing fidelity, automating tasks from quality control to facial recognition. The true paradigm shift, however, lies in the
advent of multimodal AI and generative pretrained transformers (GPTs). Live
video conversations with GPTs became widely used, giving entrepreneurs and
companies an eye of an expert/consultant in marketing, finance, investment,
human resource (HR) management, etc. throughout the day, even for simple
decisions (Liu et al., 2024). These advanced systems are moving beyond single-sense data processing, enabling AI to understand and create across diverse
modalities – text, image, audio, video – simultaneously. This integration of
sensory perception gives rise to “synthetic perception systems,” pushing the
boundaries of what machines can comprehend and produce and unlocking
unprecedented avenues for innovation (Herrmann, 2023). Imagine AI not
just generating a marketing slogan but designing the accompanying visual,

2.2. Cryptographic Security

At the heart of blockchain’s integrity lies cryptography, the science of secure
communication. Every piece of data, every transaction, and every block on
the blockchain is secured and linked using sophisticated cryptographic algorithms, primarily hash functions and digital signatures.

A cryptographic hash function (e.g., SHA-256, used by Bitcoin) takes an
input of any size (e.g., a transaction record, a document, or even an entire
movie file) and produces a fixed size, unique string of characters – its “digital
fingerprint.” Even the slightest alteration to the input data will result in a drastically different hash output, a phenomenon known as the avalanche effect.
This makes it incredibly easy to verify data integrity: if the calculated hash
doesn’t match the recorded hash, the data have been tampered with.

Let _D_ be a block of data (e.g., transaction details). Let _H_ be a ­cryptographic
hash function. The hash of the data is _h_ = _H_ ( _D_ ) .

transaction, the sender uses their private key to encrypt a hash of the transaction data. Anyone can then use the sender’s public key to decrypt this signature and verify that it was indeed signed by the owner of the private key, and

added to a block, and that block is subsequently added to the blockchain, it
becomes virtually impossible to alter or remove. This permanence is achieved
through the sequential linking of blocks via their cryptographic hashes. Each
block contains:

1. A list of validated transactions.

2. A timestamp of its creation.

3. The hash of the _previous_ block.

4. Its own unique hash.

If a malicious actor attempts to alter a transaction within an old block,
the hash of that block would change. Because the next block’s hash includes
the altered hash of the previous block, the entire chain would instantly break,
invalidating all subsequent blocks. This structural integrity, combined with
the distributed nature (where every node holds a copy), makes tampering
practically impossible without re-computing the hashes for every subsequent
block on most of the network’s nodes – a computationally infeasible task for
any single entity.

In a decentralized network with no central authority, how do all these
independent nodes agree on the single, correct version of the ledger? This is
where consensus mechanisms come into play. These are the algorithms that

ensure all participants in the network arrive at the same agreement about
the validity of transactions and the order of blocks, preventing issues like
“double-spending” (where the same digital asset is spent twice).

Proof of work (PoW) and proof of stake (PoS) are two consensus mechanisms securing blockchain networks. PoW keeps things secure by using
computer power – miners solve tough puzzles that need lots of energy and
special gear. PoS works differently: it uses money as the safety net. Validators
lock up some of their crypto, which uses way less energy and allows faster
transactions. Bitcoin and Litecoin use PoW. PoS is used by Ethereum (after
the Merge), Cardano, and Solana. These differences impact sustainability,
decentralization, security models, and the inclusivity of participation, making
each suitable for various applications such as financial services, decentralized

5. CHALLENGES AND ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

In a world full of bold claims about smooth digital change, tech-savvy entrepreneurs know true innovation is rarely easy. Blockchain offers a gamechanging path to build trust and show transparency, but using it broadly
brings loads of tech troubles, rules to follow, and knotty moral puzzles. This
part dives into those hurdles, giving entrepreneurs a crisp look at what they’re
really up against with decentralized systems – a veritable maze of moving

move. Every problem could lead to a clever solution.

5.1. Scalability

One of blockchain’s most persistent Achilles’ heels has been its scalability.
That’s its ability to handle tons of transactions per second (TPS) while still
staying decentralized and secure. Early public blockchains, like Bitcoin and
Ethereum (before its “Merge”), really struggled with this, leading to slow
transaction times and costly fees. For businesses to really use blockchain,
needing thousands, or even millions, of transactions daily, these limits become
huge roadblocks.

Blockchain developers often refer to the “blockchain trilemma,” which
posits that a blockchain can only achieve two out of three desirable properties simultaneously: decentralization, security, and scalability (Equation 4.2).
Compromising one for the other is a constant balancing act.

Blockchain Trilemma = Decentralization ∧ Security ∧ Scalability (4.2)

Entrepreneurs must critically evaluate a blockchain’s underlying scalability
solutions when choosing a platform. A highly scalable private or consortium
blockchain might offer immediate throughput for an enterprise solution but
at the potential cost of lesser decentralization.

5.2. Regulatory Uncertainty

Blockchain tech is evolving fast – way faster than the legal and regulatory
rules around the world (similar to other tech). This unclear landscape brings
big risks and tangled problems for entrepreneurs trying to build legal, lasting
businesses in the space. It’s a thicket that demands sharp thinking and real
foresight.

Rules vary a lot from country to country, creating a split-up environment.
What’s legal in one place (like a security token offering) might be banned
or tightly controlled in another. This “regulatory arbitrage” brings chances
to earn but also big compliance headaches for global businesses. Regulators
still wrestle with how to label blockchain assets (e.g., is a token a security, a
commodity, or a utility?), which affects what rules apply (e.g., securities laws
or money transfer licenses). No clear labels confusion – a true mare’s nest for
cross-border innovation.

While blockchain offers transparency, the pseudonymous nature of public
addresses can complicate AML and KYC compliance for service providers.
Regulatory bodies are increasingly mandating robust identity verification
mechanisms for platforms interacting with fiat currencies.

For tech-savvy entrepreneurs, engaging with legal counsel knowledgeable
in digital assets and monitoring global regulatory shifts is as crucial as technical development.

5.3. Energy Consumption and Environmental Consequences

The environmental impact of some blockchain networks – especially those
using PoW – has raised big ethical and sustainability questions.

Bitcoin’s PoW system is super secure, but it eats up a huge amount of electricity. By 2025, Bitcoin mining is expected to release 98 million metric tons of
CO2 – about the same as Qatar – and use as much electricity as Poland, along
with water on par with Switzerland (McGovern, 2025). This is a serious issue
for entrepreneurs and investors who care about sustainability.

The industry is rapidly migrating toward more energy-efficient consensus
mechanisms. Ethereum’s successful transition to PoS in 2022 (the “Merge”)
reduced its energy consumption by over 99.9% (Coinaute, 2025), setting a
new standard. Newer blockchains are predominantly built on PoS or other
energy-efficient protocols.

Entrepreneurs building on blockchain must prioritize platforms with
sustainable consensus mechanisms or develop strategies to mitigate their

environmental impact to meet growing environmental, social, and governance
(ESG) demands from consumers and investors.

5.4. Ethical and Societal Dilemmas

Beyond the technical and regulatory challenges, blockchain’s unique properties give rise to profound ethical and societal considerations that entrepreneurs must grapple with responsibly.

Public blockchains give pseudonymity – addresses are visible but not
always linked to real people. This can be misused for shady acts like money
laundering, funding terror, and ransomware deals. Finding the right balance
between privacy for honest users and power for law enforcement to fight
crime is a constant ethical tightrope – a true conundrum that resists easy
fixes. The demand for privacy-preserving technologies like ZKPs is growing to achieve this balance. Furthermore, the immutability of blockchain
records fundamentally clashes with concepts like Europe’s General Data
Protection Regulation and the “right to be forgotten.” Storing personally
identifiable information directly on a public blockchain raises significant
compliance issues. Even though decentralization sounds really promising,
real-world situations often end up becoming centralized. Think about those
huge mining pools in PoW, or how just a few folks own most of the stakes
in PoS. And don’t forget the power held by a handful of big-shot developers or the companies that run the core stuff (like node providers or Remote
Procedure Call (RPC) services) – these things can really erode the whole
decentralization idea. Entrepreneurs need to build solutions that push for
true decentralization, not just some airy-fairy theory.

Blockchain can totally amp up financial inclusion, but using it means you
need internet, some tech know-how, and often a good chunk of cash (for staking or fees). This can actually widen the digital divide, making communities
without solid tech fall even further behind. Entrepreneurs really need to build
tools that are simple and easy for anyone to use, shrinking those lofty entry
barricades – a tough problem that needs some serious smarts.

Once smart contracts are live, changing their code becomes very difficult. Bugs or security holes are major financial losses in DeFi attacks.
Thorough checks and formal audits are critical. Fixing these issues
requires more than technical skills – it also needs a clear understanding of
market conditions, laws, and ethics. For tech-smart founders, these issues
aren’t obstacles but opportunities for innovation, influencing the future
of digital trust.

2.1. Virtual Reality

VR plunges users into entirely simulated, immersive digital environments. It
replaces the real world with a virtual one, typically achieved through headmounted displays that block out physical surroundings and provide a stereoscopic (3D) view. The goal is to achieve “presence” – the psychological
sensation of truly being in a virtual place.

The efficacy of VR relies on minimizing latency ( _L_ ), the delay between a
user’s action and the corresponding visual response. A high latency can induce
motion sickness and break immersion. Ideally, _L_ ≤ 20 ms for a comfortable
experience, though values closer to 7 ms are considered optimal for full
immersion (Wang et al., 2023). This requires powerful Graphics Processing
Units (GPUs), efficient rendering pipelines, and low-latency tracking systems.

From bulky, tethered headsets, VR is rapidly moving toward lighter, standalone devices. Consider the advancements in pancake optics that allow for
slimmer profiles and inside-out tracking that eliminates external sensors.
Next-generation haptic feedback suits and gloves, like the HaptX G1 Gloves
showcased at Consumer Electronics Show (CES) 2025, are moving beyond
simple vibrations to simulate complex tactile sensations such as pressure, texture, and even temperature, through microfluidic actuators and pneumatic
systems (HaptX, 2025). This enriches the haptic fidelity of the experience, a
crucial component in bridging the physical and digital. AI tools can now autocreate massive virtual worlds, detailed buildings, and Non-Player Characters
(NPCs) that act believably – cutting down both cost and build time. Picture
a founder designing a digital concert hall to AI can instantly fill it with thousands of lifelike avatars, each behaving differently and reacting in real time.

5. CHALLENGES AND THE FUTURE OF THE METAVERSE

The metaverse, though full of promise for creators, isn’t a smooth, perfect
dream. Its big goal – one connected digital world – faces serious tech, moral,
and social roadblocks. For sharp-minded builders, knowing these challenges
isn’t a red flag, it’s a smart strategy. Spotting them early helps shape tough
business plans, fair rules, and creative fixes that could guide the metaverse’s
long-term impact. As we stretch the edge of digital life, we also face the weight
of building a brand-new digital frontier.

First, privacy is a huge worry – made worse by how deeply the metaverse
pulls people in. Unlike old-school websites, these spaces collect loads of personal info: biometric data from VR (like eye movement, pupil size, gaze direction,
full-body motion), physical reactions, and subtle behavior (what people stare
at, click on, or feel). This tiny data can reveal mood, thoughts, or even health
issues – opening big moral questions about tracking and control. Example: an

of the metaverse unusable for many. Third, strong rules and regulations are
now urgently needed. The metaverse runs in a legal gray zone, where today’s
laws don’t really fit these open, digital spaces. With no clear rules, it could turn

its features and strengths. But here’s the twist – new research in entrepreneurship says it’s not just about the company or outside factors. Business decisions
dig deeper, needing models that also zoom in on personal traits – those little
quirks and unique stuff about the people behind the biz.

The macro blueprint (x-Macro) is the big-picture tech game plan – the
heavyweight stuff that makes adoption even possible. We’re talking cloud
platforms, huge global IoT webs, or sleek AI systems humming quietly in the
background. Swing over to the micro attributes (x-Micro). These are the closeup details – the quirks that make each tech tick. They seriously shape how
startups actually roll this tech into their daily grind and how well it sticks.

3. METHODOLOGY

This study adopts a quantitative research design, aiming to empirically examine the role of emerging digital technologies – namely AI, IoT, and blockchain

- in fostering sustainable innovation and resilience among entrepreneurial
ventures. Data were collected using a structured questionnaire administered
to tech-savvy entrepreneurs in Jordan. This methodological choice aligns with
prior research emphasizing the efficacy of quantitative approaches for identifying measurable relationships and testing hypotheses in entrepreneurial and
innovation contexts (Vogt, 2017).

To ensure that respondents were indeed technologically proficient and had
adopted digital tools in their ventures, three filter questions were included at
the beginning of the questionnaire:

1. Do you currently use or have you previously implemented digital
technologies (e.g., AI, IoT, blockchain) in your business operations?

2. Do you actively follow updates and trends related to emerging
technologies in your industry?

3. Have you been involved in decision-making related to adopting digital
tools in your business?

Only those who answered “Yes” to all three questions were allowed to
proceed, ensuring relevance and validity of responses.

The final sample size was _n_ = 152 respondents, a figure considered
­adequate for partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM),
especially given the model complexity and the need for robust path analysis
(­Mehmetoglu & Venturini, 2021). Data collection was conducted from March
to June 2025, using Google Forms as the distribution platform. The questionnaire was administered in English, with a welcoming message explaining the
purpose of the study and assuring confidentiality. It consisted of three main
sections:

1. Demographic information.

2. Evaluation items (utilizing a 5-point Likert scale).

3. Open-ended questions for qualitative insights.

The questionnaire items were adapted from validated instruments in existing literature, ensuring content reliability and construct validity (Podsakoff

This study set out to explore how emerging digital tech – AI, IoT, and blockchain – impacts two major sustainability goals for startups: shrinking carbon
footprints and boosting sustainable innovation and resilience. The research
leaned on a data-driven approach, using structured surveys filled out by techsavvy entrepreneurs. To dig into the results, we applied SEM, which let us
rigorously test six key hypotheses about how these technologies directly shape
greener practices and innovation performance.

The results are clear: AI, IoT, and blockchain all make a strong, positive
difference when it comes to cutting carbon emissions and driving sustainable
innovation and resilience. AI came out on top – it’s the heavyweight here.
It sharpens up operations and helps shape business models that can bend
without breaking. IoT brought solid value too, with real-time environmental
tracking and smart automation that keeps things running smoothly and sustainably. Together, this tech trio forms a kind of sustainability syzygy. Blockchain’s clear, unchangeable record-keeping really boosts accountability and
teamwork when it comes to innovation. And here’s the kicker – it’s not just
theory anymore. These results back up frameworks like TOE and the ­extended
TxOEI, but more importantly, they give entrepreneurs and policymakers real,
useful tips to weave sustainability right into their digital business plans.

However, the study has limitations that warrant consideration. The crosssectional design restricts causal inferences and captures only a snapshot in
time; longitudinal research would better elucidate the dynamics of technology
adoption and sustainability outcomes. Focusing only on tech-savvy business
